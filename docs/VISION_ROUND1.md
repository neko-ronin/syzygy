This is a design judgement for the next build, not a certification of the
renderer. The complete Round One response remains available in this
conversation. D1 and D2 below preserve its full wording, with image links made
relative to this document. They were recovered from that response rather than
reconstructed from recollection; there is no missing detail in these passages.
D5, D6 and D7 are the new decisions requested in Round Two.

The cited images were opened again this round. The existing section sheets are
named `B-cold-industrial-sheet.jpg`, `A-saturated-sheet.jpg`,
`D-verdant-sheet.jpg` and `C-fire-lit-sheet.jpg`; those are the sheets examined.
The references are curated video extracts. No source MOV was decoded in either

No captures, comparison runs, dev server, browser, code edits or numeric

## D1 — Recovered: inhabitation comes from an occupied route with readable destinations

In [B's snow-ropeway frame](../references/sections/B-cold-industrial/03-snow-ropeway.jpg),
nearby walkers establish human scale; the eye then follows a crowded, pale
avenue between luminous commercial edges. Heads, shoulders, and gaps between
bodies remain distinguishable against the ground. Buildings enclose that
activity rather than merely standing behind it.

[A's cablecar frame](../references/sections/A-saturated/02-cablecar.jpg) makes
the same relationship explicit: a warm curved deck carries walking groups,
separated silhouettes, and long shadows through cool vertical depth. The crowd
follows the architecture.

My target is **busy destinations connected by visible pedestrian streams**,
with quieter approaches between them. Walking together is supported by the
frames; browsing and waiting at market thresholds are my proposed extension.
Light should reveal shoulders, clothing planes, feet, and ground contact while
preserving silhouette separation. Concentrate occupation around entrances,
counters, stairs, and transit landings.

Our [street output](../shots/2026-09-29T19-30-34/01c-street-crowd.png) lacks that
enclosing frontage and occupied route. Making pedestrians visible will expose
this larger compositional gap.

## D2 — Recovered: night needs luminous activity that illuminates its surroundings

B's snow-ropeway frame supplies the commercial avenue structure;
[C's canyon frame](../references/sections/C-fire-lit/04-canyon.jpg) supplies
lamps accompanying occupied walking decks. Neither is a matching full-night A
market reference. Also, `A-saturated/03-funicular-night.jpg` visibly reads as
daylight despite its filename.

For A's night, my design ruling is: **the market aisle becomes the frame's
luminous subject**. Shop interiors and lanterns must reveal counter fronts,
canopy undersides, pavement, and passing bodies. Warm light should continue
through successive thresholds into depth, with shadowed interruptions that
preserve individual pools. Signs announce destinations; windows above suggest
occupation beyond the street.

The eye should find the nearest illuminated human activity, follow the aisle,
then discover higher windows and cable cars against cool atmospheric depth.
This gives the requested brightness inversion a visible purpose.

In our [night frame](../shots/2026-09-29T19-30-34/08-night-lanterns.png), red lines
attract attention without revealing a place beneath them. The missing
relationship is **source → illuminated surface → occupant**.

## D5 — The hero: Market Spine Counter Walk, entering the covered vendor row at night

**I agree that the market aisle is the strongest candidate.** It brings the
inhabitation problem and the received-light problem into the same human-scale
composition. The existing
[daytime market view](../shots/2026-10-01T16-10-49/09-market-down-day.png)
shows the place we can build from: opposing stall rows, an open walking surface,
lantern cords overhead and tall enclosure beyond. Its
[night counterpart](../shots/2026-10-01T16-10-49/09-market-down.png)
shows why merely having those objects is insufficient: a foreground ground wash
does not make the counter frontage or the continuing route readable.

I name the selected route **Market Spine Counter Walk**. This is a design name
assigned here to a specific part of the existing market, not a claim that the
generator already gives streets proper names. It is the walking strip beside
the roadward faces of the first complete right-hand stall run ahead of the
existing `09-market-down` anchor, following that preset's viewing direction.
The route runs from the uncovered approach gap into the covered vendor
frontage, then out through the next uncovered gap. The open passage is the
through-route; the counters under the awnings are its destinations. I am not
inventing a nearby tram station or claiming a district boundary at either end.

The camera stands in that run's approach gap at standing adult eye height,
beside the stall-front walking strip. It looks along the row towards its far
gap, following the existing market-down heading. The nearest counter is on the
right, its front and side visible; the open aisle and opposing row remain on
the left. A slight view into the counter faces must retain their depth. The
camera is level enough to keep the walking surface, bodies and frontage as
the subject. Towers and overhead cords give enclosure without taking over the
frame. The phase is night, with A's warm/cool palette.

The existing centreline pose identifies the market segment; it is not an
approved hero composition. Select the run and camera from the actual emitted
stall geometry and connected walking route. Preserve the world identity of the
existing market-down set when selecting it, and record the resulting subject
and pose for subsequent reviews. The supplied images do not prove that a crowd
route already traverses this strip. That connection is a requirement of the
target, not an asserted feature. Moving a camera to a distant crowd and calling
the result this market would change the subject.

**What must remain readable through the frame:**

- **Foreground:** the nearest counter reads as something a person can approach.
  Its top, support, stock or working surface, awning edge and lantern mounting
  have distinct silhouettes. The paving beneath the nearest bodies retains
  joints and ground contact in the warm light. Heads, shoulders and separated
  leg shapes establish human scale beside the counter. Cloth reads as hanging
  material; timber and metal retain different material reads.
- **Middle distance:** passing pedestrians continue along an unobstructed route,
  while bodies facing the counters establish a destination beside that flow.
  Groups remain separate from the stall supports. Lantern and interior light
  reveal successive frontage faces as well as pavement. Shadowed interruptions
  separate illuminated places without severing the route. Awnings and signs
  vary in silhouette enough that the row reads as adjoining businesses.
- **Distance:** the far gap remains an opening that the walking route reaches.
  Occupation continues towards it; the crowd does not stop at the foreground.
  Awning edges and lit thresholds still describe the street's direction before
  detail settles into cool haze. Large architectural masses recede behind this
  activity rather than breaking it into a field of thin bright lines.

The route hierarchy comes from
[B's snow-ropeway frame](../references/sections/B-cold-industrial/03-snow-ropeway.jpg),
where nearby walkers lead into an occupied avenue. Its clear walking-surface
silhouettes also come from
[A's cablecar frame](../references/sections/A-saturated/02-cablecar.jpg).
Those are structural references, not matching night-market exposures. The
counter-side viewpoint and night treatment are authored design decisions.

This aisle qualifies as the hero only when it is genuinely walkable, populated
and connected to its counter destinations, and when received warm light makes
that relationship readable into depth.

**Disqualifiers — each rejects the hero in either phase:**

- **People read as street construction or repeated upright markers:** the
  bodies merge into a continuous ribbon or repeat the same upright stance in
  a fence-like row or mechanical field. Their collective silhouette reads as
  an edge, support rhythm or arranged stock rather than independent people
  occupying and using the selected walking strip. This fails even when heads,
  clothing panels and feet can be identified, and the bodies are drawn, lit
  and continued into depth. Judge at the intended whole-frame viewing size;
  identifying the meshes in a magnified crop cannot approve the frame.
- **Empty central slab surrounded by scenery:** the principal route reads as
  unused floor with market objects at its edges. Open carriageway is acceptable
  when the occupied counter-front walk remains the subject.
- **Bodies hidden behind counters:** roofs, stock or continuous counter bands
  conceal people instead of revealing approach and use.
- **Foreground clump followed by vacancy:** near occupation gives way to an
  empty middle distance before the walking route reaches its exit.
- **Luminous fixtures over black frontage:** light sources are visible, but
  their pavement, counter faces and passing bodies are not readable.
- **Overhead view concealing ground contact:** roofs and skyline carry the
  picture while the viewer cannot locate bodies on the walking surface.
- **A tightly cropped attractive stall concealing the continuing street:**
  close material detail replaces the occupied route the hero must expose.

Neither straight alignment nor a shared waiting pose alone is disqualifying.
A queue can qualify when its people remain readable and visibly belong to a
destination. Random scatter or cosmetic pose variation cannot substitute for
occupation belonging to the route. These clauses apply independently to both
members of the pair in [HERO_FRAME_SPEC.md](HERO_FRAME_SPEC.md), which binds
the comparison to the same subjects before phase changes. The crowd clause
was originally added after the captured-band review; it is now inside the
list and explicitly covers repeated stance. See
[the specification clarification](VISION_ROUND8.md).

An aerial cable-car vista is the more immediately photogenic choice. It would
let atmospheric depth and skyline silhouette carry a frame while the missing
human-scale relationships remained unresolved. This counter walk exposes the
shared systems the team most needs to establish.

## D6 — C can be specified: an inhabited, weathered vertical quarter under amber backlight

**Yes. The sheet plus a design decision is sufficient to specify C.** My
decision is that "fire-lit" describes the amber impression of the light. The
district remains inhabited. Literal burning and collapse are not established
by the opened references.

In [C's fiery-aerial frame](../references/sections/C-fire-lit/01-fiery-aerial.jpg),
warm horizon light meets cool architectural shade; occupied bridges and planted
ledges connect the towers. In
[C's cables frame](../references/sections/C-fire-lit/03-cables.jpg), projecting
rooms, service fittings, hanging fabric and foliage accumulate around tall
cores, with warm light catching their faces. In
[C's canyon frame](../references/sections/C-fire-lit/04-canyon.jpg), amber
atmospheric depth and illuminated walking decks thread through dark irregular
masses. These are the primary references for C's target.
[C's cablecar frame](../references/sections/C-fire-lit/02-cablecar.jpg) supplies
the vertical cable and bridge relationships, but its cool light is a foil to
the chosen amber register, not another palette to average into it.

**Palette and value structure.** Use ochre, weathered cream, rust red and brown
on the architectural surfaces. Amber and honey light catch projecting edges,
walking decks and window reveals. Recesses gather into charcoal and cool
blue-grey shadow; isolated cool interior accents can remain. Keep the near
wall, rail and human silhouettes dark enough to frame the luminous canyon
opening. Separate the inhabited decks from the deep shadowed building masses,
then let those masses lose detail into warm atmospheric depth. Orange on every
surface would erase both the material read and the light direction. This is
the hierarchy visible in the cables and canyon frames, translated into an
authored palette.

**Silhouette and base/podium.** C's signature is accretion: box rooms, roofs,
balconies, brackets and planted ledges project unevenly around tall cores.
Walking decks wrap around those projections, cross the void, and disappear
behind them. Open slots and deep recesses interrupt the mass. At the base,
stairs, covered entries and occupied ledges must continue that assembly down
to a readable landing, rather than leave a tower perched over an empty slab.
The cables frame grounds the projecting-room grammar; the canyon frame grounds
the wrapping decks and near stair landing. Extending that occupied assembly
to the street base is my design prescription.

This gives C a ragged, layered edge and deep pockets of shade. Against the
opened sheets, A reads through clean chromatic blocks, B through cold massive
enclosure, and D through broad planted terraces. C must remain recognisable
through its irregular assembled silhouette even before the amber light is
applied. Recolouring the present terracotta tiers cannot provide that.

**Light source and what it falls on.** A low warm sky opening behind or across
the canyon supplies the dominant backlight and grazing side light. It catches
room projections, roof lips, suspended cabins, foliage edges and people on
exposed decks. Local lamps and occupied interiors reveal deck surfaces, steps
and window recesses in the shade. The cables frame supports the warm grazing
light; the canyon frame supports the luminous walking routes. Haze reveals
depth between these layers. There is no default fire emitter below the city,
no blanket ember emissive on masonry, and no requirement for billowing smoke.

**Cables and ropeways.** Main ropeway lines should cross the tall void with
suspended cabins that read independently against haze. Local service cables
cluster near projecting rooms and their attachment points, then sag across
gaps. Cloth hangs from some of those local connections. Foreground cables
create strong dark diagonals; finer lines recede between the buildings. Leave
open air around the principal crossing so a cabin, a bridge and its walkers
do not merge into a wire screen. This follows the cables frame's long lines
and the cablecar frame's repeated cabins across depth. Cable density belongs
to connections between places, not a uniform cage around every tower.

**Material read.** Specify patched plaster or painted masonry, oxidised metal
frames, weathered timber, dull glass, and hanging cloth. Service boxes, pipes,
window casings and rail connections give the close surfaces construction and
use. These are authored material choices grounded in the worn walls, fittings,
recessed windows and hanging fabric visible in the cables frame. Warm edges
must read as surfaces receiving light. Dark streaking can express weathering;
it must not silently become evidence of fire damage. Trees and planting remain
local additions to the assembled buildings, as in the fiery-aerial frame,
rather than D's continuous garden terraces.

**Effect on the written contract.** This decision replaces the burning-ruin
premise for the C target proposed here. `DESIGN.md`, R11 and `sections.json`
still contain that premise; they have not been edited in this document-only
round. Engineers should not treat their soot, collapse, ember and smoke list
as requirements of this design. C still needs new form grammar, but the lack
of a ruin renderer is not its design blocker. If literal destruction is wanted
as a separate direction, it needs an explicit brief and a reference that shows
it; the current sheet cannot substantiate that choice. Full source-video
inspection remains outstanding, so this ruling does not claim that fire is
absent from every unsampled moment of the videos.

## D7 — The inhabitation judgement after the pedestrian rendering fix

The deciding things are **readable grounded bodies**, **occupation belonging to
the route**, and **occupation continuing into depth**. Missing any of these
makes the inhabitation judgement `more work needed`, regardless of palette,
cable density or skyline spectacle.

**Readable grounded bodies.** I must see human silhouettes that separate from
each other, from stall supports, and from the ground. Nearby heads and
shoulders establish bodies; feet, occlusion and shading establish where they
stand. Clothing can take warm light while a shadowed figure remains readable
against lit paving. Faces are not required to explain the crowd. In
[A's cablecar frame](../references/sections/A-saturated/02-cablecar.jpg), the
near walkers and their shadows provide this human scale. A merged dark bar,
floating figure or repetition that reads as fence posts fails this condition.

**Occupation belonging to the route.** The people must visibly use the walking
surface and its destinations. A passing stream occupies the clear aisle;
grouped or counter-facing bodies belong beside the frontage. There must be
room to recognise both relationships. A's cablecar frame shows people
following the deck and its bend;
[B's snow-ropeway frame](../references/sections/B-cold-industrial/03-snow-ropeway.jpg)
shows a crowd occupying the avenue between commercial edges. Counter-facing
occupation is the market extension specified here. A scatter across an empty
slab or bodies intersecting counters fails even if individual models render
cleanly. A still can establish posture, grouping and placement; it cannot
certify gait, avoidance or actual browsing behaviour.

**Occupation continuing into depth.** The near people must connect visually
to occupied middle distance and a busy destination farther along the route.
The crowd may gather and loosen around frontage, but the avenue must not turn
empty just beyond the nearest group. Light and atmospheric depth must preserve
that continuity before distant bodies resolve into collective activity. B's
snow-ropeway frame is the direct reference: foreground walkers connect to the
crowded avenue that continues into haze. A convincing foreground cluster over
an otherwise vacant street fails this condition.

**The first failure I most expect is a crowd that becomes visible as a
counter-like band.** This is a prediction, not a diagnosis of the current
raster fault. Overlapping bodies, repeated poses or poor separation from the
stall fronts could form a dark comb with coloured tops. The
[existing daytime market image](../shots/market_row-afternoon.png) contains
ambiguous bar-shaped foreground masses; I am not claiming those prove that
the currently broken pedestrian path renders. Their ambiguity is exactly the
read to reject. The useful recognition test is whether the eye sees people
using counters, or sees another set of rectangular street objects. Visible
pixels alone cannot close the inhabitation gap.

Judge the phase actually supplied. A daylight bug-fix frame can pass these
inhabitation conditions without night warmth, but it does not approve the
night hero. A frame from another street can demonstrate visible pedestrians,
but cannot certify Market Spine Counter Walk. The hero also has to satisfy
D5's frontage, received-light and material relationships. No frame produced
after the pending fix was available for this round's judgement.

**Round reflection.** This is still the requested design task: the recovered
reasoning is written down, the hero has a reproducible subject, C has an
explicit direction, and the next crowd frame has decisive failure conditions.
The output is a build target awaiting a frame, not another characterisation
round or a claim that the current city is approved.

FIRST APPROVED FRAME: Market Spine Counter Walk — from the open approach into the first complete right-hand covered stall run ahead of `09-market-down`, at standing eye height, looking along that row towards its next open gap at night.

THE THREE DECIDING THINGS:

Readable, separate human silhouettes visibly grounded beside the counters.  
Occupation that uses the clear aisle and its frontage destinations.  
An occupied route continuing from the foreground through middle distance into haze.
